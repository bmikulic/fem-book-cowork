\section{Direct Formulation of the Beam Element}
\label{sec:beam_direct}
% =============================================================================

The Euler-Bernoulli beam element models transverse bending under the assumption
that plane cross-sections remain plane and perpendicular to the neutral axis
(no shear deformation). Consider a two-node element of length $L$, bending
stiffness $EI$, with nodes~1 and~2 as shown in \cref{fig:beam_element}.

\subsection{Element Kinematics}

The Euler-Bernoulli hypothesis states that the transverse displacement $v(x)$
completely describes the element state. The curvature and bending moment are:
\begin{equation}
    \kappa(x) = \frac{\dd^2 v}{\dd x^2}, \qquad
    M(x)      = EI\,\kappa(x) = EI\,\frac{\dd^2 v}{\dd x^2} ,
    \label{eq:beam_curvature}
\end{equation}
and the shear force is $V(x) = \dd M/\dd x = EI\,v'''(x)$.
The governing strong-form equation for a distributed transverse load $q(x)$
(positive upward) is:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\dd^2}{\dd x^2}\!\left(EI\,\frac{\dd^2 v}{\dd x^2}\right) = q(x),
    \qquad 0 < x < L .
    \label{eq:beam_de}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Stiffness Matrix from the Unit Displacement Method}

The element has four DOFs:
$\vect{u}^e = [v_1,\,\theta_1,\,v_2,\,\theta_2]\transpose$,
where $v_i = v(x_i)$ and $\theta_i = v'(x_i)$.
The $j$-th column of $\matr{k}^e_b$ gives the nodal forces produced when DOF~$j$
is set to unity and all others are zero, while the element is in a state of
pure bending (no distributed load, $q=0$).

For unit displacement $v_1 = 1$ ($\theta_1 = v_2 = \theta_2 = 0$), the
displacement field is the unique cubic satisfying these four boundary
conditions:
\[
    v(x) = 1 - 3\xi^2 + 2\xi^3, \qquad \xi = x/L .
\]
The moment and shear distributions follow from $M = EI v''$ and
$V = EI v'''$, and the four nodal force components are evaluated at
$x=0$ and $x=L$. Repeating this procedure for each of the four unit
DOFs and assembling the results gives the $4\times4$ element stiffness:
\begin{equation}
    \matr{k}^e_b = \frac{EI}{L^3}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        12  &  6L  & -12  &  6L \\
        6L  & 4L^2 & -6L  & 2L^2 \\
       -12  & -6L  &  12  & -6L \\
        6L  & 2L^2 & -6L  & 4L^2
    \end{bmatrix}
    \label{eq:beam_stiffness}
\end{equation}

\begin{importantbox}
\begin{equation*}
    \matr{k}^e_b = \frac{EI}{L^3}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        12  &  6L  & -12  &  6L \\
        6L  & 4L^2 & -6L  & 2L^2 \\
       -12  & -6L  &  12  & -6L \\
        6L  & 2L^2 & -6L  & 4L^2
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation*}
\end{importantbox}

\subsection{Unit Displacement Interpretation}

The columns of $\matr{k}^e_b$ carry physical meaning analogous to the bar.
Setting $v_1 = 1$ with $\theta_1 = v_2 = \theta_2 = 0$ requires forces
$V_1 = 12EI/L^3$ (upward at node~1), $M_1 = 6EI/L^2$ (CCW),
$V_2 = -12EI/L^3$ (downward at node~2), $M_2 = 6EI/L^2$ (CCW) —
exactly the first column of $\matr{k}^e_b$.

\begin{remark}
The beam stiffness matrix is symmetric (Maxwell's reciprocal theorem) and
singular: a uniform translation $v_1 = v_2 = c$, $\theta_1 = \theta_2 = 0$
or a rigid rotation produce no curvature and hence no forces. The matrix
has a two-dimensional null space corresponding to the two rigid-body modes
of the unconstrained beam element.
\end{remark}

\section{Displacement Field Approximation}
\label{sec:beam_displacement}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Hermite Shape Functions}

A unique cubic polynomial is determined by four conditions: values and slopes
at both endpoints. Setting $v(0) = 1$, $v'(0) = 0$, $v(L) = 0$, $v'(L) = 0$
gives $H_1$; similarly for $H_2$, $H_3$, $H_4$. With $\xi = x/L$:
\begin{align}
    H_1(\xi) &= 1 - 3\xi^2 + 2\xi^3 , &
    H_2(\xi) &= L\bigl(\xi - 2\xi^2 + \xi^3\bigr) , \notag \\
    H_3(\xi) &= 3\xi^2 - 2\xi^3 , &
    H_4(\xi) &= L\bigl(-\xi^2 + \xi^3\bigr) .
    \label{eq:hermite}
\end{align}
The displacement field within the element is:
\begin{equation}
    v(x) = H_1 v_1 + H_2 \theta_1 + H_3 v_2 + H_4 \theta_2
         = \vect{H}(x)\,\vect{u}^e .
    \label{eq:beam_disp}
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}
The Hermite functions satisfy the Kronecker delta property at the nodes
($H_i(x_j) = \delta_{ij}$ for displacement DOFs, analogously for rotations)
and ensure $C^1$ continuity: both $v$ and $v' = \theta$ are continuous across
element boundaries. This is essential for the Euler-Bernoulli beam, whose
bending energy requires one derivative of $v$ to be square-integrable.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Curvature-Displacement Matrix}

Differentiating \cref{eq:beam_disp} twice,
\begin{equation}
    \kappa(x) = \frac{\dd^2 v}{\dd x^2}
    = \matr{B}_b(x)\,\vect{u}^e ,
    \qquad
    \matr{B}_b = \frac{1}{L^2}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \dfrac{12x}{L} - 6 , &
        6\dfrac{x}{L} - 4  , &
        6 - \dfrac{12x}{L} , &
        6\dfrac{x}{L} - 2
    \end{bmatrix} .
    \label{eq:beam_Bb}
\end{equation}
The bending moment follows as $M(x) = EI\,\matr{B}_b\,\vect{u}^e$.

\subsection{Enrichment by a Particular Solution}

The Hermite cubic \cref{eq:beam_disp} satisfies the homogeneous beam equation
($q = 0$) exactly but represents only a linear moment distribution. Under
a distributed load $q(x) \neq 0$, the exact deflection field includes higher
polynomial terms that the cubic cannot capture.

Analogous to the bar element enrichment, augment the field with a particular
solution $v_p(x)$:
\begin{equation}
    v(x) = \vect{H}(x)\,\vect{u}^e + v_p(x) ,
    \label{eq:beam_disp_enriched}
\end{equation}
where $v_p(x)$ satisfies the inhomogeneous equation with zero endpoint
displacements and slopes:
\begin{equation}
    EI\,v_p'''' = q(x), \qquad
    v_p(0) = v_p'(0) = v_p(L) = v_p'(L) = 0 .
    \label{eq:beam_vp_bvp}
\end{equation}
For uniform upward load $q(x) = q_0$, integrating four times and applying the
four boundary conditions gives:
\begin{equation}
    v_p(x) = \frac{q_0}{24EI}\,x^2(L - x)^2 .
    \label{eq:beam_vp}
\end{equation}
One can verify that $EI v_p'''' = q_0$ and all four endpoint conditions are
satisfied. The enriched field is nodally exact: the FEM solution at the nodes
is unaffected (since $v_p$ vanishes there), but the interior fields correctly
represent the quartic deflection under uniform load.

\begin{remark}
For downward load of intensity $\bar{q} > 0$ (upward-positive convention:
$q_0 = -\bar{q}$), the particular solution becomes
$v_p = -\bar{q}\,x^2(L-x)^2 / (24EI) < 0$ (downward deflection).
\end{remark}

\section{Element Load Vector}
% =============================================================================

For a distributed transverse load $q(x)$ (upward positive), the consistent
element load vector is
\begin{equation}
    \vect{f}^e = \int_0^L \vect{H}(x)\transpose q(x)\,\dd x .
    \label{eq:beam_load_integral}
\end{equation}
Evaluating for uniform $q(x) = q_0$:
\begin{align}
    f^e_1 &= \int_0^L H_1\,q_0\,\dd x = \frac{q_0 L}{2} , &
    f^e_2 &= \int_0^L H_2\,q_0\,\dd x = \frac{q_0 L^2}{12} , \notag \\
    f^e_3 &= \int_0^L H_3\,q_0\,\dd x = \frac{q_0 L}{2} , &
    f^e_4 &= \int_0^L H_4\,q_0\,\dd x = -\frac{q_0 L^2}{12} ,
\end{align}
so that
\begin{equation}
    \vect{f}^e = \frac{q_0 L}{12}
    \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ L \\ 6 \\ -L \end{bmatrix} .
    \label{eq:beam_load}
\end{equation}
For downward load $\bar{q} > 0$, set $q_0 = -\bar{q}$ to obtain equivalent
downward nodal forces $\mp\bar{q}L/2$ and moments.

\section{Global Assembly}
\label{sec:beam_assembly}
% =============================================================================

A beam mesh with $n$ nodes has $2n$ DOFs: $[v_1, \theta_1, v_2, \theta_2,
\ldots, v_n, \theta_n]$. The global stiffness matrix is assembled exactly as
for bar elements: each element's $4\times4$ $\matr{k}^e_b$ is added into the
global matrix at the four DOFs corresponding to nodes $i$ and $j = i+1$.
For element $e$ connecting nodes $i$ and $i+1$, the DOF mapping is
\begin{equation}
    \text{local DOFs}\ [1,2,3,4]
    \;\longleftrightarrow\;
    \text{global DOFs}\ [2i-1,\;2i,\;2(i+1)-1,\;2(i+1)] .
    \label{eq:beam_assembly}
\end{equation}
Overlapping entries (shared between adjacent elements) are added, yielding
the banded symmetric global stiffness $\stiff$.

% =============================================================================
\section{Boundary Conditions}
\label{sec:beam_bc}
% =============================================================================

Essential (kinematic) boundary conditions prescribe specific DOF values:

\begin{description}
\item[Simply supported / roller:] $v_i = 0$, $\theta_i$ free. No moment
  resistance; the rotation adjusts freely to equilibrium.
\item[Clamped (fixed):] $v_i = 0$ and $\theta_i = 0$. Both displacement and
  rotation are constrained; full moment resistance.
\item[Free end:] no essential BC imposed. Shear and moment at the free end
  are natural BCs, automatically satisfied by the variational formulation.
\end{description}

Essential BCs are applied by striking the rows and columns corresponding to
prescribed DOFs from the global system, or equivalently by the penalty method:
add a large stiffness $\alpha \gg \|\stiff\|$ to $K_{ii}$ and set $F_i =
\alpha\,\bar{u}_i$ for prescribed value $\bar{u}_i$.

\section{Worked Example: Simply Supported Beam}
% =============================================================================

\begin{example}[Two-element simply supported beam]
\label{ex:beam2}

A beam with total length $L = 2\ \text{m}$, bending stiffness
$EI = 10^4\ \text{N\,m}^2$, simply supported at both ends (nodes~1
and~3), with a central point load $P = 1000\ \text{N}$ downward at
node~2. Discretize with two equal elements of length $L_e = 1\ \text{m}$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{DOF ordering:}
$[v_1, \theta_1, v_2, \theta_2, v_3, \theta_3] = \text{DOFs}\ [1,2,3,4,5,6]$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 1: Element stiffness matrix.}

Both elements are identical ($L_e = 1\ \text{m}$). With
$K_0 = EI/L_e^3 = 10^4\ \text{N/m}$:
\[
    \matr{k}^e_b = K_0
    \begin{bmatrix}
        12 &  6 & -12 &  6 \\
         6 &  4 &  -6 &  2 \\
       -12 & -6 &  12 & -6 \\
         6 &  2 &  -6 &  4
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \text{N/m} \\ \text{N} \\ \text{N/m} \\ \text{N}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \;(\text{and N\,m for moment rows/cols}) .
\]

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 2: Global assembly.}

Element~1 occupies DOFs $[1,2,3,4]$; element~2 occupies $[3,4,5,6]$.
The graphical assembly is shown in \cref{fig:beam_simple_K_assembly};
the resulting $6\times 6$ global stiffness, divided by $K_0$, is
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\stiff}{K_0} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        12  &  6  & -12 &   6 &   0 &   0 \\
         6  &  4  &  -6 &   2 &   0 &   0 \\
       -12  & -6  &  24 &   0 & -12 &   6 \\
         6  &  2  &   0 &   8 &  -6 &   2 \\
         0  &  0  & -12 &  -6 &  12 &  -6 \\
         0  &  0  &   6 &   2 &  -6 &   4
    \end{bmatrix} .
\end{equation*}
The $2\times2$ overlap at DOFs 3--4 receives contributions from both
elements, giving $K_{33} = 12+12 = 24$, $K_{34} = -6+6 = 0$, and
$K_{44} = 4+4 = 8$ (in units of $K_0$).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 3: Load vector and boundary conditions.}

Applied loads: $F_3 = -P = -1000\ \text{N}$ (downward at DOF~3), all
other loads zero.
Essential BCs: $v_1 = 0$ (DOF~1) and $v_3 = 0$ (DOF~5). Free DOFs:
$[\theta_1, v_2, \theta_2, \theta_3]$ = DOFs $[2, 3, 4, 6]$.

The reduced $4\times4$ system:
\begin{equation}
    K_0
    \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -6 &  2 &  0 \\
                   -6 & 24 &  0 &  6 \\
                    2 &  0 &  8 &  2 \\
                    0 &  6 &  2 &  4 \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} \theta_1 \\ v_2 \\ \theta_2 \\ \theta_3 \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -P \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} .
    \label{eq:beam2_reduced}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 4: Solution (MATLAB-verified, \Cref{lst:beam_simple}).}

\begin{align*}
    \theta_1 &= -0.025\ \text{rad}, &
    v_2      &= -16.67\ \text{mm} \quad (\text{downward}), \\
    \theta_2 &= 0\ \text{rad},    &
    \theta_3 &= +0.025\ \text{rad}.
\end{align*}
By symmetry, $\theta_2 = 0$ exactly. The FEM result is nodally exact
because the loading is a concentrated nodal force:
\[
    |v_2| = \frac{PL^3}{48EI}
          = \frac{1000 \times 2^3}{48 \times 10^4}
          = 0.01667\ \text{m}
          = \mathbf{16.67\ \text{mm}} .
\]

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 5: Reactions and moment diagram.}

The support reactions follow from $\stiff\,\vect{u} = \vect{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}$:
\[
    R_1 = R_3 = 500\ \text{N} \quad
    (\textstyle\sum F = 500 + 500 - 1000 = 0\ \checkmark).
\]
The maximum bending moment occurs at midspan (node~2):
\[
    M_{\max} = \frac{PL}{4} = \frac{1000 \times 2}{4} = 500\ \text{N\,m}
    \quad (\text{sagging}) .
\]
The MATLAB script \Cref{lst:beam_simple} plots the deflection and moment
diagram (\cref{fig:beam_simple_results}).

\section{Principle of Virtual Work for the Beam}
\label{sec:beam_pvw}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Statement of the Principle}

\begin{importantbox}
\textbf{Principle of Virtual Work for the Beam:}
For a beam in static equilibrium, and for any \emph{kinematically admissible}
virtual displacement field $\delta v(x)$ (continuous with continuous slope,
and $\delta v = \delta v' = 0$ wherever $v$ or $v'$ is prescribed):
\begin{equation}
    \delta W_{\mathrm{int}} = \delta W_{\mathrm{ext}} ,
    \label{eq:beam_pvw_statement}
\end{equation}
with
\[
    \delta W_{\mathrm{int}}
    = \int_0^L M(x)\,\delta\kappa(x)\,\dd x
    = \int_0^L EI\,\frac{\dd^2 v}{\dd x^2}\,\frac{\dd^2 \delta v}{\dd x^2}\,\dd x ,
\]
\[
    \delta W_{\mathrm{ext}}
    = \int_0^L q(x)\,\delta v\,\dd x
    + \sum_i F_i\,\delta v(x_i)
    + \sum_j M_j\,\delta v'(x_j) .
\]
\end{importantbox}

\subsection{Derivation from Equilibrium}

Starting from the strong form \cref{eq:beam_de}, multiply by an arbitrary
virtual displacement $\delta v(x)$ and integrate:
\[
    \int_0^L \bigl(EI\,v''\bigr)''\delta v\,\dd x = \int_0^L q\,\delta v\,\dd x .
\]
Integration by parts once (on the left):
\begin{equation}
    \int_0^L \bigl(EI\,v''\bigr)'\delta v\,\dd x
    = \bigl[(EI\,v'')'\ \delta v\bigr]_0^L
      - \int_0^L \bigl(EI\,v''\bigr)'\,\delta v'\,\dd x .
    \label{eq:beam_pvw_ibp1}
\end{equation}
Integration by parts a second time (on the remaining integral):
\begin{equation}
    \int_0^L \bigl(EI\,v''\bigr)'\,\delta v'\,\dd x
    = \bigl[EI\,v''\,\delta v'\bigr]_0^L
      - \int_0^L EI\,v''\,\delta v''\,\dd x .
    \label{eq:beam_pvw_ibp2}
\end{equation}
Combining and rearranging:
\begin{equation}
    \int_0^L EI\,v''\,\delta v''\,\dd x
    = \int_0^L q\,\delta v\,\dd x
    + \bigl[(EI\,v'')'\,\delta v\bigr]_0^L
    - \bigl[EI\,v''\,\delta v'\bigr]_0^L .
    \label{eq:beam_pvw_final}
\end{equation}
The boundary terms yield the natural boundary conditions: $V = (EIv'')'$
(shear) at a transverse-free end contributes to $\delta W_{\mathrm{ext}}$ via
$V\,\delta v$, and $M = EI v''$ (moment) at a rotationally-free end via
$M\,\delta v'$. At nodes where displacements or rotations are prescribed,
admissibility forces $\delta v = 0$ or $\delta v' = 0$, respectively,
so those boundary terms vanish. This recovers \cref{eq:beam_pvw_statement}
exactly.

\begin{remark}
Equation~\cref{eq:beam_pvw_final} is the \emph{weak form} of the beam
equation. The strong form \cref{eq:beam_de} requires $v$ to be
four-times differentiable; the weak form requires only that $v''$ and
$\delta v''$ are square-integrable. Hermite cubic shape functions satisfy
this requirement with their $C^1$ continuity, making them the natural
choice for beam finite elements.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Recovery of the Beam Stiffness Matrix}

Substitute the Hermite interpolation $v = \vect{H}\,\vect{u}^e$,
$\delta v = \vect{H}\,\delta\vect{u}^e$, $v'' = \matr{B}_b\,\vect{u}^e$
into the left-hand side of \cref{eq:beam_pvw_final}:
\[
    \delta\vect{u}^{e\transpose}
    \!\left[\int_0^L EI\,\matr{B}_b\transpose\matr{B}_b\,\dd x\right]
    \!\vect{u}^e
    = \delta\vect{u}^{e\transpose}\,\matr{k}^e_b\,\vect{u}^e ,
\]
giving:
\begin{equation}
    \matr{k}^e_b = \int_0^L EI\,\matr{B}_b\transpose\matr{B}_b\,\dd x
    = \frac{EI}{L^3}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        12  &  6L  & -12  &  6L \\
        6L  & 4L^2 & -6L  & 2L^2 \\
       -12  & -6L  &  12  & -6L \\
        6L  & 2L^2 & -6L  & 4L^2
    \end{bmatrix} ,
    \label{eq:beam_pvw_ke}
\end{equation}
identical to \cref{eq:beam_stiffness}. The right-hand side of
\cref{eq:beam_pvw_final} produces $\vect{f}^e = \int_0^L \vect{H}\transpose q\,\dd x$,
recovering \cref{eq:beam_load}.

\section{Galerkin Finite Element Formulation of the Beam}
\label{sec:beam_galerkin}
% =============================================================================

\begin{remark}
\Cref{ch:weighted_residuals} applied the Galerkin method with a single
global polynomial to the axially loaded bar. The formulation here uses the
Hermite shape functions $H_1$--$H_4$ as both trial and test functions on
each element — the standard Bubnov--Galerkin approach for the beam.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Weighted Residual Statement}

Substitute the Hermite trial $v^h = \vect{H}\,\vect{u}^e$ into the strong
form residual:
\begin{equation}
    R(x) = \bigl(EI\,v^{h\prime\prime}\bigr)'' - q(x) .
    \label{eq:beam_galerkin_residual}
\end{equation}
Since the Hermite cubics have zero fourth derivative, $v^{h\prime\prime\prime\prime} = 0$,
so $R = -q(x)$ — the same situation as for the bar. The Galerkin statement
requires the residual to be orthogonal to each test (shape) function:
\begin{equation}
    \int_0^L H_i(x)\,R(x)\,\dd x = 0, \qquad i = 1,2,3,4 .
    \label{eq:beam_galerkin_statement}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Integration by Parts to the Weak Form}

Substituting $R = (EI v^{h\prime\prime})'' - q$ and integrating by parts
twice (identical to \cref{eq:beam_pvw_ibp1,eq:beam_pvw_ibp2}):
\begin{equation}
    \int_0^L EI\,H_i''\,v^{h\prime\prime}\,\dd x
    = \int_0^L H_i\,q\,\dd x + F_i^{\mathrm{natural}} ,
    \label{eq:beam_galerkin_weak_i}
\end{equation}
where $F_i^{\mathrm{natural}}$ collects the shear/moment boundary terms.
Writing all four rows simultaneously:
\begin{equation}
    \underbrace{\left[\int_0^L EI\,\matr{B}_b\transpose\matr{B}_b\,\dd x\right]}_{\matr{k}^e_b}
    \!\vect{u}^e
    = \underbrace{\int_0^L \vect{H}\transpose q\,\dd x}_{\vect{f}^e} .
    \label{eq:beam_galerkin_system}
\end{equation}

\begin{importantbox}
The Galerkin FE formulation yields the same element matrices as direct
derivation:
\[
    \matr{k}^e_b = \int_0^L EI\,\matr{B}_b\transpose\matr{B}_b\,\dd x
    = \frac{EI}{L^3}\begin{bmatrix}
        12 & 6L & -12 & 6L \\ 6L & 4L^2 & -6L & 2L^2 \\
       -12 & -6L & 12 & -6L \\ 6L & 2L^2 & -6L & 4L^2
    \end{bmatrix} ,
    \quad
    \vect{f}^e = \int_0^L \vect{H}\transpose q\,\dd x
    = \frac{q_0 L}{12}\begin{bmatrix}6\\L\\6\\-L\end{bmatrix} .
\]
\end{importantbox}

\section{Equivalence of Galerkin and Virtual Work Formulations}
\label{sec:beam_galerkin_pvw}
% =============================================================================

The Galerkin weak form \cref{eq:beam_galerkin_weak_i} and the PVW equation
\cref{eq:beam_pvw_final} are identical statements. Setting $w_i = \delta v$:
\begin{align}
    \text{PVW:}\quad
    &\int_0^L EI\,\frac{\dd^2 v}{\dd x^2}\,\frac{\dd^2 \delta v}{\dd x^2}\,\dd x
     = \int_0^L q\,\delta v\,\dd x + \sum_i F_i\,\delta v(x_i), \notag \\[4pt]
    \text{Galerkin:}\quad
    &\int_0^L EI\,v^{h\prime\prime}\,w_i''\,\dd x
     = \int_0^L q\,w_i\,\dd x + F_i^{\mathrm{natural}} .
    \label{eq:beam_galerkin_pvw_comparison}
\end{align}
Under $w_i = \delta v$ the two are identical: both enforce the same integral
condition over every kinematically admissible test field.

\begin{remark}
An important distinction for beams: the test functions $w_i = H_i$ and the
virtual displacements $\delta v$ must be $C^1$ (continuous with continuous
slope), because the weak form involves $w_i''$. This is more demanding than
for bar elements ($C^0$ suffices) and is why Hermite cubics — rather than
linear Lagrange polynomials — are required. The Galerkin method's mandate to
choose test functions from the same space as the trial directly prescribes
this continuity requirement.
\end{remark}

\section{Principle of Minimum Potential Energy}
\label{sec:beam_pmpe}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Total Potential Energy of the Beam}

\begin{definition}
The \textbf{bending strain energy} of a linearly elastic beam is
\begin{equation}
    U = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L EI\!\left(\frac{\dd^2 v}{\dd x^2}\right)^{\!2}\dd x .
    \label{eq:beam_pmpe_strain_energy}
\end{equation}
The \textbf{total potential energy} is
\begin{equation}
    \Pi[v] = U - W_{\mathrm{ext}},
    \qquad
    W_{\mathrm{ext}} = \int_0^L q\,v\,\dd x + \sum_i F_i\,v(x_i)
                                               + \sum_j M_j\,v'(x_j) .
    \label{eq:beam_pmpe_tpe}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\subsection{Stationarity Condition and the Weak Form}
\label{sec:beam_pmpe_stationarity}

\begin{importantbox}
\textbf{Principle of Minimum Potential Energy (PMPE):} Of all kinematically
admissible displacement fields, the one satisfying equilibrium makes $\Pi$
stationary:
\begin{equation}
    \delta\Pi = 0
\end{equation}
for every admissible variation $\delta v$. For a stable elastic equilibrium,
$\Pi$ is a global minimum.
\end{importantbox}

Taking the first variation of \cref{eq:beam_pmpe_tpe}:
\begin{equation}
    \delta\Pi
    = \int_0^L EI\,v''\,\delta v''\,\dd x
      - \int_0^L q\,\delta v\,\dd x
      - \sum_i F_i\,\delta v(x_i) - \sum_j M_j\,\delta v'(x_j)
      = 0 .
    \label{eq:beam_pmpe_first_variation}
\end{equation}
Identifying $\delta W_{\mathrm{int}} = \int EI v'' \delta v''\,\dd x$ and
$\delta W_{\mathrm{ext}}$ from the remaining terms, this is precisely the
PVW statement \cref{eq:beam_pvw_statement}:
\begin{equation}
    \delta\Pi = 0
    \;\Longleftrightarrow\;
    \delta W_{\mathrm{int}} = \delta W_{\mathrm{ext}} .
    \label{eq:beam_pmpe_pvw_link}
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}
PMPE is a special case of PVW for conservative (elastic) systems. Both
principles are equivalent for the Euler-Bernoulli beam with fixed applied
loads. PMPE provides the variational foundation that guarantees PVW holds;
PVW generalizes to non-conservative systems (e.g.\ aerodynamic follower
loads) where no potential energy functional exists.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Element Stiffness Matrix and Load Vector from PMPE}
\label{sec:beam_pmpe_matrices}

Substitute $v = \vect{H}\,\vect{u}^e$, $v'' = \matr{B}_b\,\vect{u}^e$
into the element bending energy:
\[
    U^e = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L EI\,\vect{u}^{e\transpose}
          \matr{B}_b\transpose\matr{B}_b\,\vect{u}^e\,\dd x
        = \frac{1}{2}\,\vect{u}^{e\transpose}
          \!\underbrace{\left[\int_0^L EI\,\matr{B}_b\transpose\matr{B}_b\,\dd x\right]}_{\matr{k}^e_b}
          \!\vect{u}^e .
\]
The external work term gives $W^e_{\mathrm{ext}} = \vect{u}^{e\transpose}\vect{f}^e$,
so the element potential energy is the quadratic:
\begin{equation}
    \Pi^e = \frac{1}{2}\,\vect{u}^{e\transpose}\matr{k}^e_b\,\vect{u}^e
            - \vect{u}^{e\transpose}\vect{f}^e .
    \label{eq:beam_pmpe_tpe_element}
\end{equation}
Stationarity $\partial\Pi^e/\partial\vect{u}^e = \vect{0}$ gives
$\matr{k}^e_b\,\vect{u}^e = \vect{f}^e$, recovering \cref{eq:beam_stiffness}
and \cref{eq:beam_load}.

\begin{importantbox}
Minimizing the element potential energy yields the stiffness matrix:
\[
    \matr{k}^e_b
    = \int_0^L EI\,\matr{B}_b\transpose\matr{B}_b\,\dd x
    = \frac{EI}{L^3}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        12 & 6L & -12 & 6L \\ 6L & 4L^2 & -6L & 2L^2 \\
       -12 & -6L & 12 & -6L \\ 6L & 2L^2 & -6L & 4L^2
    \end{bmatrix} .
\]
The stiffness matrix is the Hessian of the bending strain energy with
respect to the nodal DOFs.
\end{importantbox}

\subsection{Summary: Four Equivalent Paths to the Beam Element Matrices}
\label{sec:beam_pmpe_summary}

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\small

\section{Natural Coordinates for the Beam Element}
\label{sec:beam_natural}
% =============================================================================

For generality and numerical integration, map $x \in [0, L]$ to
$\xi \in [0, 1]$ via $x = \xi L$, $\dd x = L\,\dd\xi$. The Hermite functions
in natural coordinates are:
\begin{align}
    \tilde{H}_1(\xi) &= 1 - 3\xi^2 + 2\xi^3 , &
    \tilde{H}_2(\xi) &= \xi - 2\xi^2 + \xi^3 , \notag \\
    \tilde{H}_3(\xi) &= 3\xi^2 - 2\xi^3 , &
    \tilde{H}_4(\xi) &= -\xi^2 + \xi^3 ,
    \label{eq:beam_hermite_natural}
\end{align}
so that $H_1(x) = \tilde{H}_1(\xi)$ and $H_2(x) = L\tilde{H}_2(\xi)$, etc.

The second derivative transforms via the chain rule:
$\dd^2 v/\dd x^2 = (1/L^2)\,\dd^2 v/\dd \xi^2$.
The stiffness integral in natural coordinates becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \matr{k}^e_b = \int_0^1 EI\,\tilde{\matr{B}}_b(\xi)\transpose
                  \tilde{\matr{B}}_b(\xi)\,\frac{\dd\xi}{L^3} ,
    \label{eq:beam_isomap}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\matr{B}}_b = (1/L^2)[d^2\tilde{H}_i/d\xi^2]$ collects the
curvature shape derivatives. Since $\tilde{H}_i$ are cubic polynomials,
$\tilde{\matr{B}}_b\transpose \tilde{\matr{B}}_b$ is a polynomial of degree~2
in $\xi$; a 2-point Gauss--Legendre quadrature (mapped to $[0,1]$) integrates
it exactly. This is the foundation for numerical integration of beam and frame
elements with variable $EI(x)$.
